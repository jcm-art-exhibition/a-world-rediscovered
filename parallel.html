The Parallel Exhibit

Same World, Different Voices

Biblical texts emerged within a world already full of stories, laws, images, and ideas. When we place them beside other writings from the ancient Near East, familiar patterns begin to surface.

The Parallel is designed to make that shared cultural world visible. You’ll read biblical passages alongside related ancient texts, noticing where their language, structure, and ideas resemble one another and where they begin to diverge.

Similarity does not necessarily mean direct borrowing, and difference does not always tell us why a writer made a particular choice. But together they can help us see the larger conversation biblical writers inherited, participated in, and reshaped. The invitation is to notice both what is shared and what becomes distinctive.

The Parallel Exhibit — Creation & Chaos

Enūma Eliš
&
Genesis 1

Scroll ↓
Stratum I — The Waters
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts open with the same picture: a formless, watery deep before anything exists.

Genesis even keeps the word — tehom shares a root with Tiamat’s name — but leaves the goddess out of it.

Stratum II — The Split
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts picture the world emerging as waters are divided into an ordered cosmos.

Genesis shares the same world-picture, but replaces divine conflict with the effortless speech of one God.

Stratum III — The Lights
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts describe the sky’s lights by the job they do, not what they’re made of.

Genesis assigns the sun, moon, and stars to mark seasons and years — exactly what Enūma Eliš’s stars are for.

Stratum IV — Humanity
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts place humanity’s creation in the very same spot — the last act before the gods rest.

Genesis follows the identical sequence, even as it reshapes why humanity exists.

Stratum V — Rest
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts end the same way: a god resting once the ordered world is complete.

Genesis reframes that rest as a blessing, but the pattern — a finished cosmic house, now occupied — stays the same.

The Parallel Exhibit — The Flood

Gilgamesh XI
&
Genesis 6–9

Scroll ↓
Stratum I — The Sealing
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts seal their vessel with the same word for the substance that seals it.

Hebrew kopher and Akkadian kupru are close enough that standard lexicons cross-reference them directly — the same term for pitch, doing the same job.

Stratum II — The Boarding
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts save more than one man — a whole living system boards together.

Family, livestock, every kind of creature: scholars treat this ‘preserved microcosm’ as one of the flood tradition’s most consistent shared beats.

Stratum III — The Storm
What This Parallel Reveals

Both floods run on the same clock: trouble measured in sevens.

Every surviving Mesopotamian flood account times its storm to seven days and nights — and Genesis opens its own countdown the same way.

Stratum IV — The Dove
What This Parallel Reveals

Both heroes test the flood’s end the same way: sending a bird out to look for land.

The device itself is the parallel — this scene, or one very like it, recurs across the Mesopotamian flood tradition, Genesis included.

Stratum V — The Offering
What This Parallel Reveals

Both survivors step off the boat and do the same thing first: burn an offering the gods can smell.

Gilgamesh has the gods swarm the sacrifice ‘like flies,’ starved without it; Genesis keeps the sweet savor but quietly drops the hunger.

The Parallel Exhibit — The Covenant Code

Code of Hammurabi
&
Exodus

Scroll ↓
Stratum I — The Talion
What This Parallel Reveals

Both codes answer harm with the same measure — and the very words for what’s at stake share a root.

Hebrew ayin and Akkadian īnu, both ‘eye,’ descend from the same Proto-Semitic word — as do the terms for ‘tooth.’ The law and its vocabulary are inherited together.

Stratum II — The Ox
What This Parallel Reveals

Both codes are built from the same legal sentence — ‘if a man does X, then Y’ — and reach for the same test case.

The goring ox is one of that casuistic form’s most famous recurring examples, showing up in law collections centuries apart.

Stratum III — The Theft
What This Parallel Reveals

Both codes punish theft the same way: pay back more than what was taken.

Hammurabi’s thirtyfold and tenfold, Exodus’s fivefold and fourfold — the ratios differ, but multiplied restitution is the shared convention underneath.

Stratum IV — The Kidnapping
What This Parallel Reveals

Both codes draw the identical line: stealing property costs money, stealing a person costs a life.

Kidnapping sits apart from ordinary theft in both traditions — not a matter of degree, but of kind.

Stratum V — The Fire
What This Parallel Reveals

Both codes open this law the same way: with the fire itself, before anyone asks who’s liable.

Exodus’s opening words echo Hammurabi’s almost exactly — the same casuistic instinct, two law codes, centuries and cultures apart.
