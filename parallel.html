The Parallel Exhibit

Same World, Different Voices

Biblical texts emerged within a world already full of stories, laws, images, and ideas. When we place them beside other writings from the ancient Near East, familiar patterns begin to surface.

The Parallel is designed to make that shared cultural world visible. You’ll read biblical passages alongside related ancient texts, noticing where their language, structure, and ideas resemble one another and where they begin to diverge.

Similarity does not necessarily mean direct borrowing, and difference does not always tell us why a writer made a particular choice. But together they can help us see the larger conversation biblical writers inherited, participated in, and reshaped. The invitation is to notice both what is shared and what becomes distinctive.

The Parallel Exhibit — Creation & Chaos

Enūma Eliš
&
Genesis 1

Scroll ↓
Stratum I — The Waters
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts begin before an ordered world has taken shape, with primordial waters already in view.

The shared language of “the deep” places Genesis within a recognizable ancient world, while Genesis treats those waters not as a divine rival, but as part of the world God orders.

Stratum II — The Split
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts picture an ordered world emerging through the separation of primordial waters.

In Enūma Eliš, that order follows Marduk’s violent defeat of Tiamat. In Genesis, the same kind of cosmic separation happens by God’s command alone.

Stratum III — The Lights
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts use the lights of the sky to organize time.

Enūma Eliš places the stars in divine stations and associates them with the gods; Genesis presents the heavenly bodies as created lights with assigned roles in God’s ordered world.

Stratum IV — Humanity
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts ask what humanity is for, but they give strikingly different answers.

In Enūma Eliš, humans are created to carry the gods’ labor. In Genesis, humanity receives God’s image, blessing, and authority within creation.

Stratum V — Rest
What This Parallel Reveals

Both accounts connect the completion of cosmic order with divine rest, but the contrast is revealing.

In Enūma Eliš, humans are created to relieve the gods of their labor. In Genesis, God completes his own work, rests, and blesses the seventh day.

The Parallel Exhibit — Flood & Survival

Gilgamesh XI
&
Genesis 6–9

Scroll ↓
Stratum I — The Sealing
What This Parallel Reveals

Both flood vessels are waterproofed with pitch, and even the words for the material are closely related.

That unusually specific technical overlap places Genesis within the wider world of Mesopotamian flood tradition.

Stratum II — The Boarding
What This Parallel Reveals

Both vessels carry more than a single survivor; they preserve a small world of life through the flood.

The passenger lists differ, but in each story survival means carrying enough of the old world forward for life to continue afterward.

Stratum III — The Mountains
What This Parallel Reveals

Both flood vessels end their drifting on a mountain.

The geography changes, but the mountain landing marks the same transition: the catastrophe has passed, and the survivor is about to reenter the world.

Stratum IV — The Dove
What This Parallel Reveals

Both stories send out a dove to test whether the flood has receded.

The larger bird sequences differ, but each uses the same simple test: let a bird leave the vessel and see whether it finds a place to land.

Stratum V — The Offering
What This Parallel Reveals

Both flood stories end with sacrifice, and both describe its pleasing aroma reaching the divine.

But Gilgamesh pictures the gods gathering eagerly around the offering, while Genesis shifts the focus to God’s response and the future of the world.

The Parallel Exhibit — Storm & Kingship

Ugaritic Baal Traditions
&
Psalm 29

Scroll ↓
Stratum I — The Assembly
What This Parallel Reveals

Both texts imagine a heavenly world populated by divine beings.

Psalm 29 opens inside that familiar divine-council world, but immediately turns every voice in the assembly toward YHWH.

Stratum II — The Voice
What This Parallel Reveals

Both traditions hear divine power in the thunder of the storm.

Baal’s thunder is his “voice.” Psalm 29 takes that familiar storm image and makes the voice rolling over the waters YHWH’s.

Stratum III — The Cedars
What This Parallel Reveals

Psalm 29 sends YHWH’s storm through a northern landscape already familiar from Ugaritic tradition.

Lebanon, Sirion, and their cedars appear in Baal’s world as well. Here, that landscape moves at the voice of YHWH.

Stratum IV — The Shaking
What This Parallel Reveals

In both texts, the storm announces divine presence by making the landscape move.

Baal’s voice makes mountains tremble. Psalm 29 gives comparable earth-shaking force to the voice of YHWH.

Stratum V — The King
What This Parallel Reveals

The storm imagery ultimately makes a claim about who rules.

Baal’s storm power supports his kingship in Ugaritic tradition. Psalm 29 culminates by declaring YHWH enthroned as King forever.
